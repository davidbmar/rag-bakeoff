The retriever was never the problem

Six document-QA systems, one tax question none of them could answer, and what a day of measurement turned up.

rag-bakeoff · 13 questions · 5 IRS publications · 393k tokens

It started with a question a person would actually ask: “I had more expenses than rent on my rental house this year. Should I deduct the loss below zero? What are the pros and cons?”

That question is governed by the passive activity loss rules, the $25,000 special allowance and its phase-out, the at-risk limits, and depreciation recapture years later. It contains none of those words. Which turns out to be the whole story.

What was measured, and why not answers

For each question, the concepts you must have in front of you to answer correctly were written down in advance and verified to exist in the corpus before anything was run — so a miss is always a retrieval failure and never a gap in the source material. An engine scores on how many of those its retrieved passages actually contain.

Final answers were deliberately not judged. An LLM grader conflates two different failures: not finding the material, and mishandling material you did find. Only the first decides whether an architecture can work at all. A model cannot reason from a passage it was never shown.

Critical counts only the concepts whose absence makes the answer actively wrong rather than merely thin. Missing the passive-activity rule on the rental question does not produce a vaguer answer; it produces a confident and incorrect one.

The mechanism, in one screen

Every question that plain retrieval failed, failed the same way. Here is the loop working the rental question — round one searches the caller's own phrasing, and then: 

round 2: passive activity loss limitation rental real estate
         at-risk rules rental property losses
         rental loss deduction $25000 special allowance

round 3: passive activity loss limitation deduction ordering rules
         rental real estate active participation requirements definition
         excess business loss limitation noncorporate taxpayers

Not one of those phrases appears in the question. The model read what came back, noticed what was missing, and searched again in the corpus's own vocabulary. HyDE reaches the same place in a single shot by writing the passage it expects to exist — “…subject to the passive activity loss limitations under section 469…” — and searching with that instead of with the question.

This is the finding. The gap is query-side, not index-side. Six engines were built over the years and none of them was the problem. Asking any of them a single question phrased in the caller's vocabulary was.

Seven things worth keeping

01

Question type predicts failure; engine quality barely does

Every factual lookup passed on every engine. Every strategy, vocabulary-gap and multi-hop question failed on plain retrieval, on both engines, at every k. “Is our RAG good?” is unanswerable. “Good at which kind of question?” is the only version with a number attached.

02

Better embeddings help and do not save you

Dense beat BM25 by 17 points — real, and worth having. It still failed the question that prompted the whole exercise, scoring 14% where keyword search managed 43%. No embedding model fixes a query that does not contain the concept.

03

Lexical and dense fail on different questions

BM25 alone answered 4 of 11, dense 6, their union 7. That is the honest case for hybrid retrieval — and also its ceiling, assuming perfect fusion. Still four short of simply loading the right two publications.

04

Count the passages, or multi-round strategies flatter themselves

The loop went from 6/11 to 11/11 — but it saw 17 passages against five. At equal budget, single-shot dense scores 9/11. So +3 was just seeing more and only +2 was the loop. Any comparison that does not report passages seen is measuring context size and calling it intelligence.

05

A score that cannot discriminate cannot abstain

The production engine's normalized_score is scaled per query, so the top hit is always exactly 1.000. Its raw BM25 score for “what is the corporate tax rate for a C corporation” — a question the corpus cannot answer — came back higher than for a genuine in-corpus question. It only goes quiet on queries sharing no vocabulary at all. Nothing built on top of it can say “I don't know”, which for advice is the worst available failure.

06

Long context is the baseline everyone skips

Two publications, 80k tokens, no retrieval, no chunking, no infrastructure: 100% on every question. If the corpus fits in the window, retrieval is a premature optimisation — and it is worth checking whether it fits before building anything.

07

Six implementations existed because nothing was ever measured

With no way to tell whether a new system beat an old one, building the seventh always looked as reasonable as improving the third. The eval that settles it took a day and would have prevented years of parallel building. It is also the cheapest artifact here to keep.

So — is the answer to loop?

Not first. The ordering that falls out of the numbers:

  • Does the corpus fit in the window? Load it and stop. Nothing else here beats 100% for zero engineering.
  • If not, add HyDE. One model call in front of the retriever you already run. 10 of 11 on the same five passages as plain dense, beating single-shot retrieval at three times the context. Best ratio on the board by a distance.
  • Loop only for questions that genuinely span several rules. It buys the last question and costs 3× the passages and 6 seconds. Route to it rather than defaulting to it.
  • Take the loop's stopping signal for free. When it reports it has enough, that is also the abstention signal — the one the keyword engine is structurally incapable of producing.

What this does not tell you

  • Retrieval was scored, not answers. A system can retrieve everything and still reason badly.
  • One domain, one corpus, 13 questions. Tax guidance is unusually well-structured; narrative or conversational corpora may behave differently.
  • No reranker was tested. The literature puts cross-encoder reranking in the 15–48% range and one already exists in the portfolio, unused for documents.
  • Three of the six systems could not take this corpus at all and were excluded, with reasons, rather than quietly dropped.
  • Nothing here touches the hardest part of the original question: the answer depends on the asker's income, participation hours and basis, which are in no document. The best system asks before it answers.
