The retriever was never the problem

A tax question whose answer is nowhere in the words you would use to ask it — and five techniques measured against it to find out which ones can cross that gap.

rag-bakeoff · 13 questions · 5 IRS publications · 393k tokens

The problem

Ask a chatbot a question about your own documents and something specific happens under the hood. The system does not read all of your documents — it cannot; they do not fit. Instead it searches them, pulls back the handful of passages that look most relevant to your question, and shows only those to the language model, which then writes an answer from them.

This is called retrieval-augmented generation, and nearly every “chat with your documents” product works this way. It rests on one assumption that is almost never examined:

That the passages which look like your question are the passages that answer it.

Usually that holds. Ask “how many years do I depreciate a rental property?” and the paragraph containing the answer also contains the words “depreciate” and “rental property”. Search finds it. Everyone is happy.

This document is about the case where the assumption fails — where the passage that answers you looks nothing like the question you asked. When that happens the system does not tell you it failed. It returns five plausible, on-topic passages, the model writes a fluent answer from them, and the answer is wrong in a way you cannot see. For a question with money or legal exposure attached, that is the worst behaviour available.

A question built to break it

Suppose you own a rental house. This year its expenses ran higher than its rent, so on paper it lost money. Suppose also that your income is above the limit for writing that loss off against your salary. Should you take the loss anyway? What are the pros and cons?

The real answer runs roughly like this, and every piece of it is written down in the IRS publications:

  • Rental losses are passive. By default they cannot reduce the tax on ordinary wages at all.
  • There is an exception — a special allowance of up to $25,000 — but it phases out as income rises and is gone entirely above a threshold. The setup says you are past it, so the exception does not apply to you.
  • So the loss is suspended: not lost, but not usable now. It carries forward and is released when you eventually dispose of the property.
  • Meanwhile depreciation is not optional in the way people assume, and it comes back as recapture when you sell — which is the genuine “con” the question asks about, arriving years after the decision.

Now count the words that question and answer have in common. The asker said “rental house”, “expenses”, “write off”, “over the limit”. The documents say “passive activity”, “special allowance”, “modified adjusted gross income”, “disposition of an entire interest”, “recapture”. They share essentially nothing.

The obvious objection is that modern search does not match words, it matches meaning — that is what embeddings do, and “write off more than it earns” ought to land near “passive activity loss” in that space.

It does not, and the reason generalises. Semantic search still needs the concept to be present in the query in some form, because it can only measure similarity to what you gave it. Here the governing rule is not a paraphrase of the question. It is a rule the asker does not know exists — which is exactly why they are asking. You cannot be similar to a thing you have never heard of.

So this is not a hard question because the corpus is large or badly written. It is hard because of a structural property that any expert domain has: the person with the question uses lay words; the document with the answer uses terms of art; and knowing the term of art is most of the expertise. Tax is the example here. Medicine, law, and any technical support corpus behave the same way.

What was tested

Thirteen questions of this shape, over five US tax publications — roughly 1.6 million characters — run through five different approaches. Two of them are working internal systems built at different times for different projects; the other three are techniques layered on top. The aim was not to rank products. It was to find out which approaches can cross a vocabulary gap, and to establish that the ones which cannot, cannot — however good their index is.

The five approaches

1. Keyword search (BM25)

The classic. Score documents by which of the query's words they contain, weighting rare words more heavily than common ones. Fast, transparent, decades old, and still the default in most databases. Its weakness is obvious here: if the answer never uses your words, it cannot find the answer.

2. Semantic search (embeddings)

The modern default. A neural model converts every passage into a list of numbers — a vector — positioned so that passages meaning similar things land near each other. Your question is converted the same way, and the search returns whatever is nearest. This genuinely handles paraphrase: “car” finds “automobile”. The open question of this exercise is whether it handles something harder than paraphrase.

3. HyDE — searching with a made-up answer

HyDE stands for Hypothetical Document Embeddings, introduced in 2022 in a paper called Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels. It addresses a specific awkwardness in how semantic search works.

Embedding models place questions and documents in one shared space, but questions and documents are different kinds of writing. A question is short and interrogative. A document passage is long, declarative and full of domain vocabulary. Measuring the distance between the two is a weaker signal than measuring the distance between two passages, which is what these models are really good at.

HyDE's move is to stop comparing apples to oranges. Ask the language model to write the passage it imagines would answer the question — a fake document — and then search using that instead. Now you are comparing a passage to passages, which is the comparison the model does well.

The part that sounds wrong: the made-up answer does not have to be correct. It is never shown to anyone and never becomes the answer. It is thrown away the instant the search returns. All that is asked of it is to contain roughly the right words — and a model that knows the domain will produce “passive activity loss”, “modified adjusted gross income” and “section 469” even while getting a threshold wrong.

You are deliberately inducing a hallucination and using it as a search key. The retrieved passages, which are real, then ground the actual answer.

4. The iterative loop

Search, read what came back, work out what is still missing, search again for that, and repeat. It is what a competent researcher does, and something a single query cannot do by construction — you cannot know what you failed to find until you look at what you found. The version tested here runs at most three rounds and stops early when the model reports it has enough, which doubles as a signal that it is ready to answer.

5. No search at all

Put the relevant documents into the prompt in their entirety and let the model read everything. Modern models accept enough text that for a small corpus this is simply possible, and it removes every retrieval failure by removing retrieval. It is included as the control, because an approach that cannot beat “just read it all” is hard to justify on a corpus that fits.

What was measured, and why not answers

For each question, the concepts you must have in front of you to answer correctly were written down in advance and verified to exist in the corpus before anything was run — so a miss is always a retrieval failure and never a gap in the source material. An engine scores on how many of those its retrieved passages actually contain.

Final answers were deliberately not judged. An LLM grader conflates two different failures: not finding the material, and mishandling material you did find. Only the first decides whether an architecture can work at all. A model cannot reason from a passage it was never shown.

The mechanism, in one screen

Every question that plain retrieval failed, failed the same way. Here is the loop working the rental question — round one searches the caller's own phrasing, and then: 

round 2: passive activity loss limitation rental real estate
         at-risk rules rental property losses
         rental loss deduction $25000 special allowance

round 3: passive activity loss limitation deduction ordering rules
         rental real estate active participation requirements definition
         excess business loss limitation noncorporate taxpayers

Not one of those phrases appears in the question. The model read what came back in round one, saw the governing rule was missing, and went looking for it by name.

HyDE arrives in the same place in a single step. This is the fake answer it wrote for that question, before any searching happened: 

"You may deduct rental expenses in excess of rental income, subject to
 the passive activity loss limitations under section 469. If you actively
 participate and your modified adjusted gross income is $100,000 or less,
 you may deduct up to $25,000 against nonpassive income. Suspended
 passive losses disallowed in the current year may be carried..."

Search with that instead of the question and the right passages come back immediately. Note what happened: the draft supplied “passive activity loss”, “section 469”, “modified adjusted gross income”, “nonpassive”, “suspended” — every term the asker lacked. Then it was discarded. One extra model call converted a question phrased in lay terms into a query phrased in terms of art, which is exactly the bridge the problem needed.

This is the finding. The gap is on the question side, not the index side. Both search engines tested had a perfectly good index — the answer was sitting in it the whole time. What failed was walking up to that index once, holding the words the asker happened to use.

Seven things worth keeping

01

The kind of question predicts failure; the search engine barely does

Every factual lookup passed on every engine. Every strategy, vocabulary-gap and multi-hop question failed on plain retrieval, on both engines, at every k. “Is our RAG good?” is unanswerable. “Good at which kind of question?” is the only version with a number attached.

02

Better embeddings help and do not save you

Semantic search beat keyword search by 17 points — real, and worth having. It still failed the question that prompted the whole exercise, scoring 14% where plain keyword search managed 43%. No embedding model fixes a query that does not contain the concept.

03

Keyword and semantic search fail on different questions

Keyword search alone answered 4 of 11, semantic 6, the two combined 7. That is the honest case for hybrid retrieval — and also its ceiling, assuming perfect fusion. Still four short of simply loading the right two publications.

04

Count the passages, or repeated searching flatters itself

The loop went from 6/11 to 11/11 — but it saw 17 passages against five. At equal budget, a single semantic search scores 9/11. So +3 was just seeing more and only +2 was the loop. Any comparison that does not report passages seen is measuring context size and calling it intelligence.

05

A score that cannot discriminate cannot abstain

Two of the thirteen questions were deliberately unanswerable from this corpus, to see whether the systems could tell. The keyword engine reports a relevance score rescaled per query, so its best result is always exactly 1.000 no matter how bad it is. On the underlying raw score, “what is the corporate tax rate for a C corporation” — nowhere in these documents — scored higher than a genuine in-corpus question. It only goes quiet on a query sharing no vocabulary at all, like a question about baking bread. So it cannot distinguish “I found the answer” from “I found something tax-shaped”, and nothing built on top of it can honestly say I don't know — which for advice is the worst failure available.

06

Long context is the baseline everyone skips

Two publications, 80k tokens, no retrieval, no chunking, no infrastructure: 100% on every question. If the corpus fits in the window, retrieval is a premature optimisation — and it is worth checking whether it fits before building anything.

07

Duplicated systems are a symptom of missing measurement

The codebase this came from had accumulated six separate “ask questions about documents” implementations, sharing no code, built over about a year. None had ever been compared to another. That is the actual cause: with no way to tell whether a new one beat an existing one, starting fresh always looked as defensible as improving what was there. A question set with written-down answers takes a day to build and settles it permanently — and it is the cheapest thing here to keep, because it outlives every engine it judges.

So — is the answer to loop?

Not first. The ordering that falls out of the numbers:

  • Does the corpus fit in the window? Load it and stop. Nothing else here beats 100% for zero engineering.
  • If not, add HyDE. One model call in front of the retriever you already run. 10 of 11 on the same five passages as semantic search alone, beating single-shot retrieval at three times the context. Best ratio on the board by a distance.
  • Loop only for questions that genuinely span several rules. It buys the last question and costs 3× the passages and 6 seconds. Route to it rather than defaulting to it.
  • Take the loop's stopping signal for free. When it reports it has enough, that is also the abstention signal — the one the keyword engine is structurally incapable of producing.

What this does not tell you

  • Retrieval was scored, not answers. A system can retrieve everything and still reason badly.
  • One domain, one corpus, 13 questions. Tax guidance is unusually well-structured; narrative or conversational corpora may behave differently.
  • No reranker was tested. The literature puts cross-encoder reranking in the 15–48% range and one already exists in the portfolio, unused for documents.
  • Three of the six systems could not take this corpus at all and were excluded, with reasons, rather than quietly dropped.
  • Nothing here touches the hardest part of the original question: the answer depends on the asker's income, participation hours and basis, which are in no document. The best system asks before it answers.
